Method and assumptions

Train run. Each service is integrated over a 5 m distance grid: a backward pass applies the service braking rate into every stop and speed restriction, a forward pass applies tractive effort limited to the set acceleration. Speed restrictions hold until the whole train length has cleared them. Resistance = Davis A + B·v + C·v² plus gradient (m·g·i) and curve resistance (Röckl: 650/(R−55) N/kN for R ≥ 300 m, 500/(R−30) below), averaged over the train length.

Power. Motoring: wheel power ÷ drive efficiency (or the line-current curve × nominal voltage, scaled by effort used) + auxiliaries. Braking: electric brake effort up to the braking curve, × efficiency, capped at the regenerative power limit; friction brakes take the rest.

Network. Every second the nodal equations are solved for substation busbars, feeder taps and train positions on each track (conductor + return rail loop resistance). Substations are Thévenin sources (no-load voltage, source resistance from regulation) with a diode: they cannot take current back. Optional inverters absorb surplus regeneration above their switch-on voltage.

Trains on the network. Constant-power loads, iterated to convergence. Below the cut-back voltage, traction power reduces linearly to zero. Above the regeneration limit start, regenerated current reduces linearly to zero at the maximum voltage; the rest goes to the on-board rheostat.

Ratings. Rectifier and transformer loads are compared as the worst rolling 1-minute, 15-minute and 1-hour RMS values against rating × duty multiplier. Transformer kVA = DC power ÷ (rectifier efficiency × power factor). Conductor and feeder thermal checks use the 1-hour RMS current.

Short circuit. A bolted fault (set fault resistance) is placed every fault-step along each track with all substations feeding; the largest single-feeder contribution is compared with the breaker trip setting. Steady-state values: the rate of rise and peak need a transient study.

AC data. Per bridge (pulse ÷ 6 bridges), commutation reactance Xc = transformer reactance (uk, load loss) + network reactance (fault level, X/R) referred to the valve side. AC fault currents use IEC 60909 with voltage factor c and κ = 1.02 + 0.98·e^(−3R/X).

Short circuit, rate of rise. The same network is solved with inductances (conductor loop, feeder, rectifier 2·Lc per bridge plus any smoothing reactor) to give di/dt at t = 0; τ = Iss ÷ (di/dt), i(t) = Iss(1 − e^(−t/τ)); time to trip from the largest feeder contribution. Rail potential at the fault = If·Z(1 − e^(−γd)), Z = ½√(r/g), d = distance to the nearest TSS.

Harmonics. For each second of TSS current: overlap 1 − cos μ = √2·Xc·Id/Uv; Ih/I1 from the overlap formula for uncontrolled bridges; characteristic orders kp ± 1, other 6k ± 1 orders at the residual %; Vh = Ih·h·Xnet. The percentile value is compared with the limits (IEC 61000-3-6 uses 95 %). A common bus adds TSS with the IEC 61000-3-6 summation exponents. DC ripple: ideal p-pulse values Vn = √2·Udi0/(n² − 1), current through the source and quarter-section inductance.

EMC. Running rails of all tracks bonded, a ladder of rail resistance and rail–earth conductance every 100 m, train currents injected and TSS return currents drawn each second. Touch voltage taken as rail potential (no body or footwear resistance credit). Stray current = average current leaving the rails ÷ track length. Static field from the conductor rail and return currents (Biot–Savart); induced voltage = 2πf·M′·L·I with M′ from geometry, psophometric weighting and cable sensitivity.

Limits of this model. Train movement is not slowed by low voltage (power is cut back but the timetable holds), turnback / depot movements are not included, fault currents are first-order (no rectifier overshoot or arc dynamics), harmonics assume an ideal supply without background distortion or resonance, and EMC values are planning estimates to be confirmed by site measurement. The same engine runs in the Python edition (tps_engine.py) and gives the same numbers for the same inputs.
